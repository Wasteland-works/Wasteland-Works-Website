Field report 001

Cascade Failure by Pip Tech: from a bare-metal prototype to a living world.

Cascade Failure began as a deceptively simple challenge: make a self-contained 3D game that could launch directly on a Mac. The project rapidly grew into something larger—a playable RPG prototype, a modular vault and world editor, a reusable character pipeline and PIP Core, Pip Tech’s own game engine. Pip Tech is the dedicated game and engine branch of Wasteland Works.

This public log records the visible journey without revealing story material, private character sheets or unannounced narrative details.

From the recovered record

The world did not end in a war.

Humanity entered its final energy crisis slowly. Conventional nuclear fuel was being consumed faster than it could be replaced, while generations of radioactive waste accumulated beneath an increasingly power-hungry civilisation.

Atom Corp believed it had found a responsible answer. Its seventeenth experimental material—A17—could remain dormant until activation, power compact reactors for years and leave almost nothing behind. Buildings, industries and shelters adopted the networked system faster than the old infrastructure could be retired.

Then one reactor disappeared from the network.

What followed became known as the Cascade: a sequence of failures that crossed cities and borders faster than any government could understand. Surviving records contain an unexplained two-hour interruption—just long enough for part of humanity to reach shelter. Who created that window, and why the system resumed, are questions buried inside the world of Cascade Failure.

The build history

Progress, not perfection.

Phase 01
First playable

The room where it started

The earliest Cascade Failure build proved the core loop: first-person movement, aiming, interaction, dialogue, weapons, health and a working interface inside a hand-built 3D renderer. That technology evolved into the PIP Core engine. The geometry was deliberately primitive; the important result was that the game existed and could be played.

Phase 02
Systems

A game begins to form

Saving, companion selection, doors, inventory, weapon hotkeys, ambient NPC dialogue and the wrist-mounted PID Companion were layered onto the prototype. The PID became Cascade Failure’s own diegetic interface and targeting assistant rather than an imitation of another game’s device.

Phase 03
World building

The vault editor becomes a game of its own

A grid editor grew into a modular world-building tool with exterior terrain, vault entrances, automatic floors and walls, interior partitions, room zones, furniture placement, NPC occupations, 3D walkthroughs and a dedicated development vault. The long-term goal is to let players construct and inhabit multiple vaults across the wasteland.

Phase 04
Characters

Replacing primitives with performers

Characters moved from assembled shapes to higher-density humanoid meshes. A common skeleton and animation contract now supports walking, running, combat tests, inverse kinematics, equipment sockets and future facial animation. The current Jessica runtime stands at a verified 1.65 metres and retains its animation data inside the editor.

Phase 05
Equipment

Clothing and machinery

Reusable clothing was developed as real removable geometry, not painted-on colour. Lab-coat tailoring led into fitted vault suits and layered armour. In parallel, powered armour evolved from a rough silhouette into a full industrial machine built around an internal frame and removable protective sections.

Now
Integration

Bringing the pieces together

The present work centres on integrating the higher-fidelity animated cast into the Map Builder, improving environmental terrain and preparing a clean asset pipeline that can eventually target macOS, SteamOS/Linux and Windows. Cascade Failure remains in active development; everything shown here is work in progress.
